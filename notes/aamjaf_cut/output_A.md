# 1. Introduction

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) scores decide which firms ESG-screened investors can hold. A fund restricted to scored stocks, an index built from scored constituents, or a mandate with a minimum score cannot buy a firm that no provider covers. In five Southeast Asian equity markets, most listed firms had no score from the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) at any point between 2014 and 2024. Much of the academic literature on ESG ratings uses LSEG scores (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Dobrick et al., 2023). Entry into the scored universe therefore raises two questions: which firms get scored, and does entry change how the market values them?

Theory provides no single answer to the second question. Investor recognition predicts a gain for every newly scored firm, because a first score makes it visible to ESG-screening investors and a broader investor base raises its price (Merton, 1987). ESG preferences predict a gain for firms the score reveals as green and a loss for those it reveals as brown (Pástor et al., 2021; Pedersen et al., 2021). Evidence on index additions and rating changes, reviewed in Section 2, is mixed.

The first question matters because providers choose whom to score. They are likely to add a firm once it is large, liquid, and discloses enough to be scored, and their scores rise with firm size (Dobrick et al., 2023; Drempetic et al., 2020). If coverage follows growth in market value, a comparison of scored and unscored firms, or a regression that pools all years around coverage, attributes past growth to the score. U.S. studies link coverage by more raters to fewer ESG violations (Tsang et al., 2024), lower emissions and more ownership by high-ESG institutions (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026), and dividend increases (Tsang et al., 2025). None examines valuation in emerging markets, where most firms are unscored and coverage has expanded in waves.

We study Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand. LSEG, whose scores were earlier distributed as Refinitiv ESG and ASSET4, extended coverage in market-specific waves. This study makes three contributions to sustainable finance. First, it adds valuation and an emerging-market setting to the rating-coverage literature (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026; Tsang et al., 2024, 2025) and shows which firms one provider brought into its scored universe: outside its largest wave, firms that had just outgrown their peers. Second, it adds coverage initiation to the evidence on ratings as price-relevant information: unlike an MSCI downgrade (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022), entry into LSEG coverage was not followed by a detectable revaluation for firms with high or low initial scores. Third, it shows how selection into the scored universe can make scored firms look more valuable than the score itself made them.

# 2. Related Literature and Hypotheses

## 2.1. ESG ratings as information and certification

ESG ratings can move prices when they change what investors know or which investors may hold a stock. Evidence on rating changes points to both channels. MSCI downgrades are followed by lower ownership by U.S. funds with ESG mandates and a one-year abnormal return of approximately −2.37%, with weaker effects for upgrades (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022). Calendar-time portfolios of firms with rating changes show a similar asymmetry (Shanaev & Ghimire, 2022). Consensus ratings predict future ESG news, but disagreement among providers weakens both this predictive power and the price reaction to the news (Serafeim & Yoon, 2023). Publishing Morningstar sustainability ratings for mutual funds redirected fund flows, although any effect on the stocks those funds hold is indirect (Hartzmark & Sussman, 2019).

Certification by inclusion in a sustainability index is the closest analog to a first score. Additions to the Dow Jones Sustainability Index have limited price effects relative to similar firms outside the index (Hawn et al., 2018). A replication finds no price effect but more analyst following after addition and more long-term ownership after continued membership (Durand et al., 2019). In sell-side research, losing analyst coverage lowers prices (Kelly & Ljungqvist, 2012), and the first report on a neglected stock raises its price when the report is favorable (Demiroglu & Ryngaert, 2010).

## 2.2. ESG demand, recognition, and price pressure

Two channels predict a valuation response to a first score, and they differ in sign. Under investor recognition, a stock known to few investors trades at a discount because a small group bears its idiosyncratic risk (Merton, 1987). Recognition therefore predicts a gain for every newly scored firm. S&P 500 additions support a lasting gain: they raise prices permanently, whereas deletions do not lower them permanently (Chen et al., 2004). Under ESG preferences, investors accept lower expected returns on assets they classify as sustainable (Pástor et al., 2021; Pedersen et al., 2021). Because a first score reveals where a firm stands, this channel predicts gains for firms revealed as green and losses for those revealed as brown, and a pooled average can be close to zero when the two signs offset.

Demand shocks also shape realized prices before any rating event. The high realized returns of green assets reflect unexpected increases in ESG concerns rather than high expected returns (Pástor et al., 2022). S&P 500 inclusion raises prices (Harris & Gurel, 1986; Shleifer, 1986), and part of the increase reverses within weeks (Harris & Gurel, 1986). A run-up in value before coverage, followed by a flat path, is consistent with a demand shift that ended, with selection on growth, or with both.

## 2.3. Which firms providers score

Rating coverage is not assigned randomly. We expect providers to cover firms in the indices their clients track and firms that disclose enough to be scored, although our data do not include the provider's coverage rules. Mandatory sustainability reporting increases the information available on listed firms (Krueger et al., 2024). The provider we study gives higher scores to larger firms, a bias documented for its ASSET4 scores (Drempetic et al., 2020) and its Refinitiv scores (Dobrick et al., 2023), and it has rewritten historical scores on a large scale (Berg et al., 2020). Providers disagree about the same firm (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Christensen et al., 2022), and the disagreement is priced (Avramov et al., 2022; Gibson Brandon et al., 2021). If providers select firms that have grown, a comparison of scored and unscored firms mixes the effect of coverage with the growth that led to it.

## 2.4. Hypotheses

The pre-analysis plan states three hypotheses, all tested two-sided.

H~1~ (primary): The initiation of LSEG ESG coverage changes the log MTB ratio of scored firms relative to comparable never-scored firms. The recognition channel predicts an increase. The preference channel predicts an increase for high-score firms and a decrease for low-score firms. The selection view predicts relative growth before coverage and, after it, no effect if the growth was permanent or a decline if it was transient.

H~2~ (secondary): Coverage changes log market capitalization, book leverage, and log total assets.

H~3~ (heterogeneity): The effect on log MTB differs between firms with initial scores above and below their cohort median. This test separates the preference channel from recognition because only the preference channel depends on what the score says.
