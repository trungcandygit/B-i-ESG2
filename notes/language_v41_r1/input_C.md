## 5.7. Economic magnitude

Under assumption (2), the largest increase compatible with the headline interval is of the same order as the announcement return of 4.86 percent that Demiroglu and Ryngaert (2010) report for the first analyst coverage of neglected stocks, and twice the one-year abnormal return that follows an MSCI downgrade (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022). Annual year-end ratios would miss short-lived price pressure, so our estimates concern lasting revaluation of the kind a permanent rise in investor recognition would produce (Chen et al., 2004; Merton, 1987). A lasting effect half as large as the MTB run-up would lie at the upper end of the headline interval, so any such effect is small relative to the growth that preceded coverage.

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1. What the ordering of events shows

The clearest result is an ordering of events. On average, firms entered the scored universe after their MTB ratio and market capitalization had grown faster than those of never-scored firms, and their MTB ratio stopped gaining afterwards. The run-up was strongest in the Thai and Singaporean wave of 2019 and 2020 and absent in the large Malaysian wave of 2021 and 2022, which added many firms of one market within two years; in neither wave do we detect a later gain. Several mechanisms fit this ordering. The provider may have added firms once they had grown large and visible enough to matter to its clients (Section 2.3). Firms may have become scoreable by publishing a first sustainability report, joining an index, or issuing equity, any of which can coincide with rising value; the fall in leverage toward the base year is consistent with equity issuance. Firms may also have started or expanded sustainability reporting when they were doing well or planned to raise capital, a strategic-disclosure reading that our data cannot test. Each of these mechanisms implies the same lesson for empirical work: entry into a provider's coverage marks the end of a period of relative growth, not a random draw.

The flat post-coverage MTB path in the baseline admits three readings, depending on the counterfactual trend (Rambachan & Roth, 2023; Roth, 2022). With parallel paths from the base year, coverage had no effect within the interval of Section 5.2. With a continued run-up, coverage lowered valuations relative to trend. With a partly transitory run-up, the flat path is consistent with a positive coverage effect that offset a reversal. The awareness evidence on index additions predicts a permanent gain from recognition (Chen et al., 2004), so a recognition effect of that kind should have appeared as a lasting rise after coverage; we detect none under assumption (2). The publication-dated estimates in Section 5.4 show a decline in the third and fourth years of coverage for early cohorts, which a reversal would produce and a positive coverage effect could partly offset, but which could also reflect a negative effect of coverage. The evidence therefore does not show that coverage has no effect: no heterogeneity-robust specification detects a valuation gain, and the bounds do not exclude one.

## 6.2. Relation to evidence on ESG ratings and certification

The absence of a detected gain fits the evidence on certification by sustainability indices, where additions have little price effect once comparable firms are used as benchmarks (Durand et al., 2019; Hawn et al., 2018). It contrasts with the price effects of MSCI downgrades (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022; Shanaev & Ghimire, 2022). Two features of our setting may explain the difference. First, the evidence that ESG funds trade on ratings concerns MSCI, the one rating that explains their holdings (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022); entering the coverage of another provider may change little for these funds. Second, scored firms were large and visible before coverage (Table 1), and some may have had FTSE Russell scores, which leaves less room for recognition (Merton, 1987). Disagreement among providers (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Serafeim & Yoon, 2023) further weakens the information in any single score. U.S. studies find that coverage changes firm conduct (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026; Tsang et al., 2024) and payout (Tsang et al., 2025); we find no stable change in leverage or total assets and observe no measures of conduct.

The pre-coverage run-up also bears on the evidence on ESG demand. Pástor et al. (2022) show that realized green returns can reflect unexpected shifts in demand. A run-up followed by a flat path is what a one-time demand shift, or selection after one, would produce. Without ownership data, we cannot tell whether ESG investors bought these firms before or after coverage. In the Section 4.4 bound with M̄ = 0.25, the data admit MTB changes from {{mtb_rm025_lo_pct}} to {{mtb_rm025_hi_pct}} percent, so a demand shift of a few percent after coverage cannot be excluded.

## 6.3. Implications

The results have implications for five groups, each bounded by the design: one provider whose score is not the rating shown to drive ESG fund holdings, annual data, five markets, and no data on ownership, returns, or publication dates.

For rating providers and data users, the LSEG scored universe in these markets tilts toward large firms, and outside the Malaysian wave toward firms that have just outgrown their peers (Table 1, Figs. 1 and 2). Investors and researchers who use the provider's history should know that the first scored fiscal year can precede publication and that history may have been filled in, so backtests around coverage initiation may suffer from look-ahead bias (Berg et al., 2020; Sahin et al., 2023).

For exchanges, the large Malaysian wave carried no run-up (Fig. 2), which suggests that broad expansions of coverage within a market select less on prior growth than additions of individual firms. We do not observe what caused that wave, and we cannot attribute it to the November 2022 arrangement between Bursa Malaysia and LSEG (Bernama, 2022). Our data do not show whether broader coverage raises the value of the firms it reaches; in the Malaysian wave, we detect no MTB gain.

For regulators, our evidence gives no support, under assumption (2), to using rating coverage as a lever for issuer valuation. The composition of the scored universe, tilted toward firms that have already grown, is relevant to policies on coverage gaps for smaller issuers.

For ESG investors and index builders who rely on this provider, screens restricted to scored stocks admitted many firms after a valuation run-up. Our annual MTB data cannot speak to the returns of a strategy that buys newly scored firms.

For listed firms in the five markets, the estimates give no support, under assumption (2), to the expectation that a first LSEG score will raise their valuation through investor recognition or preferences (Merton, 1987; Pástor et al., 2021); the confidence interval excludes increases above {{mtb_hi_pct}} percent with the firm bootstrap and above {{mtb_cl_hi_pct}} percent with the cluster bootstrap, but smaller effects remain possible. For empirical research, a cross-sectional valuation premium of scored firms, or a static TWFE estimate, can reflect selection into coverage rather than an effect of scores (Table S8). Studies comparing scored and unscored firms in these markets should model coverage timing and report pre-coverage dynamics. These data cannot show whether the ordering extends to other markets or providers.

# 7. Limitations

The study has eight limitations. First, the provider chooses when to cover a firm, and we have no coverage variation unrelated to firm performance. The post-coverage estimates rest on conditional parallel trends from the base year, which the pre-coverage estimates question, and our sensitivity analysis uses a linear trend and a simplified bound, not the confidence sets of Rambachan and Roth (2023). Second, the data are annual and may contain backfilled or revised scores, so we observe neither first-score publication dates nor announcement returns; later dating closes only part of this gap. Third, we observe only LSEG coverage. The LSEG score is not the rating that the cited evidence links to ESG fund holdings (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022), and for firms with earlier scores from other providers, such as FTSE Russell scores for Malaysian index constituents (Bernama, 2022), the event is an additional score. We do not examine domestic ESG channels such as exchange sustainability indices or Shariah screens. Fourth, without stock returns, shares outstanding, ownership, foreign holdings, foreign-ownership limits, index membership, or firm-level sustainability report dates, we cannot separate price changes from share issuance, test the recognition channel directly, or link first scores to index entry or first sustainability reports. The reporting rules in Section 3.1 are dated at the market level from regulatory texts and, for Thailand and Malaysia, from secondary summaries, not observed for each firm. Fifth, a few market capitalization values are implausibly large; pooled winsorization limits but does not correct them, and return on assets covers a minority of firm-years. Sixth, Malaysian and Thai firms make up {{pct_my_th_treated}} percent of scored firms and dominate the pooled estimates; resampling market-by-industry clusters addresses part of the dependence within coverage waves, but five markets are too few for market-level inference. Seventh, the minimum detectable effect on log MTB is {{mtb_mde}}, about {{mtb_mde_pct}} percent, so smaller effects would be detected with less than 80% probability. Eighth, the unrecorded download date leaves the vintage of revisable recent scores uncertain.

# 8. Conclusion

Entry into LSEG ESG coverage in five Southeast Asian markets followed relative growth on average, and it was not followed by a detected rise in market-to-book ratios. Scored firms grew faster than never-scored firms of similar size, country, and industry before their first scored year, by {{mtb_runup_m5}} percent in MTB ({{mtb_runup_lo}} to {{mtb_runup_hi}} percent), while the Malaysian firms covered in the large 2021 and 2022 wave showed no such run-up. Under parallel trends, the headline effect is {{mtb_att}} (95% interval {{mtb_lo}} to {{mtb_hi}}), and we detect no difference between firms with high and low initial scores. Dating coverage one year later gives an MTB change of {{mtb_R10_pct}} percent, which may reflect a reversal of the run-up or a negative effect of coverage. Departures from parallel trends like those before coverage would admit effects of several percent in either direction. Comparisons of scored and unscored firms that ignore coverage timing can attribute this selection to the score.
