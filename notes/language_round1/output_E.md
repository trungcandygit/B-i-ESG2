# 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Interpreting the post-coverage path

The flat post-coverage MTB path admits three readings, depending on how the pre-coverage trend would have evolved (Rambachan & Roth, 2023; Roth, 2022). With parallel paths from the base year, coverage had no effect within the interval of Section 5.2. With a continued run-up, coverage lowered valuations relative to that trend. With a partly transitory run-up, the flat path is consistent with a positive coverage effect that offset a reversal. This third reading deserves weight, because firms selected after relative growth are likely selected partly on transitory value, as with index additions whose price effects partly reverse (Chen et al., 2004; Harris & Gurel, 1986).

Three results bear on the reversal reading. First, dating coverage one year later yields an MTB decline after the first scored year, which a reversal would produce and a positive coverage effect could partly offset; the estimate cannot separate the two. Second, against never-scored firms with similar pre-coverage growth, the MTB effect is small and insignificant, but this comparison conditions on a single growth measure, and its interval admits gains of several percent. Third, the bounds in Section 5.4, which follow the relative-magnitudes idea of Rambachan and Roth (2023), include zero for every M̄ we consider but also effects of several percent in both directions. The evidence therefore does not show that coverage has no effect: no specification detects a valuation gain, and the bounds do not exclude one. The most stable feature of the data is the ordering of events, since the first scored fiscal year follows a period of relative growth and coincides with a valuation peak.

The market-level estimates agree. MTB effects are close to zero in Malaysia and Thailand, the two markets with the most scored firms, and no market shows a significant positive effect. Because the coverage waves came in 2019 and 2020 in Thailand and Singapore but in 2021 and 2022 in Malaysia, a single shock common to all five markets is a less likely explanation, although the waves may reflect market-specific disclosure rules (Krueger et al., 2024; Singapore Exchange, 2016) or index changes that we do not observe.

## 6.2. Relation to prior evidence

The evidence on sell-side coverage suggests why a first ESG score might move prices: losing coverage lowers prices (Kelly & Ljungqvist, 2012), a favorable first report on a neglected stock raises them (Demiroglu & Ryngaert, 2010), and Hartzmark and Sussman (2019) document a demand channel through fund flows. Two features of ESG scores may explain why we detect no valuation gain. A score rarely carries news about cash flows, and it arrives after the firm's own disclosures. In addition, our firms were already large and visible before their first LSEG score (Table 1), and some had FTSE Russell scores, which left less room for the recognition channel of Merton (1987); foreign-ownership limits on some listed firms in these markets could further mute recognition through foreign ESG investors. We cannot test either explanation, because we observe neither ownership, investor attention, foreign holdings, nor the limits themselves.

The results also fit the evidence on rating disagreement. If ESG funds follow one provider more than others (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022), coverage by another provider may matter little for demand, and the dispersion of scores across providers (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Christensen et al., 2022) weakens the information in any single score. U.S. studies find that coverage changes firm conduct (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026; Tsang et al., 2024) and financial policy (Tsang et al., 2025); we find no stable change in leverage or assets and observe no measures of conduct.

## 6.3. Implications

The main implication is methodological. A valuation premium of rated firms in the cross-section, or a static two-way fixed effects estimate, can reflect the selection of firms into coverage rather than an effect of ratings. In our data, the static regression attributes an MTB increase of {{mtb_R6_pct}} percent to coverage, whereas the heterogeneity-robust estimator and its event-time profile trace the gap to growth before coverage (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021; Roth, 2022). Studies that compare rated and unrated firms in these markets should model the timing of coverage and report pre-coverage dynamics.

For listed firms in the five markets, the estimates give no support, under assumption (2), to the expectation that a first LSEG score will raise their market valuation through investor recognition or preferences (Merton, 1987; Pástor et al., 2021). Under that assumption, the confidence interval excludes increases above {{mtb_hi_pct}} percent, but smaller effects remain possible. The evidence does not speak to short-lived price effects around publication, to firms outside the scored sample, or to outcomes other than valuation and financing. For investors and exchanges, scored firms are a selected group with a distinct valuation history, and these data cannot show whether the same ordering holds in other markets or for other providers.

# 7. Limitations

The study has eight limitations. First, the provider chooses when to cover a firm, and we have no variation in coverage that is unrelated to firm performance. The post-coverage estimates rest on conditional parallel trends from the base year, which the pre-coverage estimates call into question, and our sensitivity analysis uses a linear trend and a simplified bound, not the confidence sets of Rambachan and Roth (2023). Second, the data are annual and may contain backfilled or revised scores, so we observe neither the publication dates of first scores nor announcement returns; dating coverage one year later closes only part of this gap. Third, we observe only LSEG coverage, and some firms already had scores from other providers, including FTSE Russell scores for Malaysian index constituents (Bernama, 2022), so for them the event is an additional score. Fourth, the data contain no stock returns, shares outstanding, ownership, foreign holdings or foreign-ownership limits, index membership, or sustainability report dates, so we cannot separate price changes from share issuance, test the recognition channel directly, or link first scores to index entry or first sustainability reports. Fifth, a few market capitalization values in the source data are implausibly large; pooled winsorization limits their influence without correcting them, an erroneous base-year value enters every group-time estimate of its cohort, and return on assets covers only a minority of firm-years. Sixth, Malaysian and Thai firms account for {{pct_my_th_treated}} percent of scored firms and carry most of the weight in the pooled estimates, and the firm-level bootstrap does not account for correlation within market-level coverage waves, so the intervals may be too narrow. Seventh, the minimum detectable effect on log MTB is {{mtb_mde}}, about {{mtb_mde_pct}} percent, so smaller effects would be detected with less than 80% probability; under parallel trends, the confidence interval excludes increases above {{mtb_hi_pct}} percent but not smaller ones. Eighth, the score download date is not recorded, so the vintage of revisable recent scores is uncertain, and we do not document the timing of disclosure rules outside Singapore.

# 8. Conclusion

Using the staggered first appearance of LSEG ESG scores for {{n_treated_all}} non-financial firms in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand, we find that scored firms, mostly Malaysian and Thai, grew faster than comparable never-scored firms before their first scored year and that, under parallel trends, their market-to-book ratios did not rise afterwards: the headline effect is {{mtb_att}} (95% interval {{mtb_lo}} to {{mtb_hi}}). Dating coverage one year later gives {{mtb_R10}}, a decline that may reflect a reversal of the run-up. The first scored year thus coincides with a valuation peak, although departures from parallel trends as large as those before coverage would admit effects of several percent in either direction. Comparisons of rated and unrated firms that ignore the timing of coverage will attribute this selection to the rating.
