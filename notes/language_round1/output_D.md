# 5. Results

## 5.1. Which firms get scored

Table 1 compares scored firms in the year before their first score with never-scored firm-years. Scored firms are much larger: their median market capitalization is {{t1_mcap_tr_med}} million U.S. dollars against {{t1_mcap_nv_med}} million, their median total assets are {{t1_asset_ratio_med}} times those of never-scored firms, and the normalized differences are {{t1_mcap_nd}} for market capitalization and {{t1_asset_nd}} for total assets. Their MTB ratio is also higher, with a mean of {{t1_mtb_tr_mean}} against {{t1_mtb_nv_mean}} and a median of {{t1_mtb_tr_med}} against {{t1_mtb_nv_med}}. Leverage barely differs, with medians of {{t1_lev_tr_med}} and {{t1_lev_nv_med}} and a normalized difference of {{t1_lev_nd}}. Mean return on assets is {{t1_roa_tr_mean}} against {{t1_roa_nv_mean}}, but it is available for far fewer firm-years, so we use it only descriptively. These gaps fit a provider that extends coverage to large and visible firms (Section 2.2), and the higher MTB level fits the pre-coverage growth reported in Section 5.3, as for firms added to an index (Chen et al., 2004). The size gap also matters for the scores themselves, because larger firms receive higher scores from this provider (Dobrick et al., 2023). These differences in levels motivate the comparison of changes and the adjustment for size, country, and industry in Eq. (3).

## 5.2. Average effects of the first score

Table 2 reports the headline estimates of Eq. (6). The effect on log MTB is {{mtb_att}}, with a standard error of {{mtb_se}} and a 95% confidence interval from {{mtb_lo}} to {{mtb_hi}}. Under assumption (2), the interval excludes changes in the MTB ratio outside the range from {{mtb_lo_pct}} to {{mtb_hi_pct}} percent. The minimum detectable effect from Eq. (7) is {{mtb_mde}}, so an effect of about {{mtb_mde_pct}} percent would be detected with 80% probability. The effects compatible with the data are given by the confidence interval, not by the MDE, and they include increases of a few percent. The effect on log market capitalization is {{mcap_att}} (Holm-adjusted p {{mcap_pholm_txt}}), and leverage rises by {{lev_att_pp}} percentage points (Holm-adjusted p {{lev_pholm_txt}}). Total assets rise by {{asset_att}} log points (unadjusted p {{asset_p_txt}}; Holm-adjusted p {{asset_pholm_txt}}). None of the four outcomes shows a significant change at the 5% level after the Holm adjustment. Under assumption (2), the MTB estimate gives no sign of the positive effect that the recognition channel of Merton (1987) and the preference channel of Pástor et al. (2021) and Pedersen et al. (2021) predict. Hartzmark and Sussman (2019) show that fund flows respond to newly published sustainability ratings; we detect no lasting valuation gain from that demand for newly scored firms in these markets.

## 5.3. Dynamics before and after the first score

Fig. 1 plots the event-time estimates of Eq. (5) for all four outcomes. Before the first score, the coefficients for market capitalization and MTB are negative and rise toward zero. In event years −5 and −4, four and three years before the base year, the market capitalization estimates are {{mcap_em5}} and {{mcap_em4}}, and the MTB estimates are {{mtb_em5}} and {{mtb_em4}} (p {{mtb_em5_p_txt}} and p {{mtb_em4_p_txt}}). Over the four years to the base year, scored firms' market capitalization grew {{mcap_runup_m5}} percent more than that of comparable firms, and their MTB ratio {{mtb_runup_m5}} percent more. This run-up resembles the growth that precedes additions to a major index (Chen et al., 2004; Harris & Gurel, 1986; Shleifer, 1986), as the selection view in Section 2.2 predicts. The joint test of Eq. (8) rejects parallel pre-trends for market capitalization (p {{mcap_pre_p_txt}}) but not at the 5% level for MTB (p {{mtb_pre_p_txt}}), and as Roth (2022) cautions, such a non-rejection is not evidence that pre-trends are absent. Total assets follow a weaker version of this pattern, and leverage falls toward the base year (pre-trend p {{lev_pre_p_txt}}).

After the first score, the MTB coefficients for event years 0 to 3 lie between {{mtb_e3}} and {{mtb_e2}}, and none differs from zero at the 5% level. For market capitalization, the event-year 1 estimate is {{mcap_e1}} (p {{mcap_e1_p_txt}}), and later estimates are not significant. Only {{mtb_nfirms_e4}} scored firms from {{mtb_ncoh_e4}} cohorts reach event year 4, so we plot that year but exclude it from Eq. (6); its estimates are {{mtb_e4}} for MTB (p {{mtb_e4_p_txt}}) and {{mcap_e4}} for market capitalization (p {{mcap_e4_p_txt}}). Total assets rise after coverage, to {{asset_e3}} in event year 3, and leverage returns toward its earlier level. The valuation gap between scored and comparable firms thus widens before coverage and stops widening afterwards, with no mirror image of the price decline that follows a loss of analyst coverage (Kelly & Ljungqvist, 2012). Falling leverage and rising assets before the base year fit firms that raise equity or grow before they enter coverage. Without data on share issuance, we cannot tell whether the pre-coverage growth in market capitalization reflects rising prices, new shares, or both, so we base statements about valuation on the MTB ratio.

## 5.4. Timing of coverage and sensitivity to pre-trends

Table 3 reports the robustness checks, including those that address the timing of coverage and the pre-trends. Row R10 dates coverage one year after the first scored fiscal year, so that the base year is the first scored year and the post-coverage window starts when a score could first have been published. Because scores were backfilled or revised (Berg et al., 2020; Sahin et al., 2023), this dating also moves the base year closer to the end of the run-up. The MTB effect is then {{mtb_R10}}, a decline of about {{mtb_R10_pct}} percent (95% interval {{mtb_R10_lo}} to {{mtb_R10_hi}}; p {{mtb_R10_p_txt}}), estimated from {{mtb_R10_ntr}} scored and {{mtb_R10_nco}} control firms. The market capitalization effect is {{mcap_R10}} (p {{mcap_R10_p_txt}}), and the asset effect is {{asset_R10}}. This estimate assumes parallel trends from the first scored year, which is the year of peak valuation. Its joint pre-trend test gives p {{mtb_R10_pre_p_txt}}, and with M̄ = 0.25 in Eq. (10) its robust interval, from {{mtb_R10_rm025_lo}} to {{mtb_R10_rm025_hi}}, includes zero; the decline is no longer significant once M̄ reaches {{mtb_R10_bd0}}. Two readings fit the decline. If the provider selects firms after a period of unusually high valuation that later partly reverses, the decline measures the reversal and can hide a positive coverage effect that offsets part of it. If valuations would otherwise have stayed at their peak, coverage lowered them. The data cannot separate the two readings.

Row R11 keeps the {{n_R11_cohorts}} cohorts first scored no later than 2021, which are observed through event year 3, with {{mtb_R11_ntr}} scored and {{mtb_R11_nco}} control firms. Its MTB effect of {{mtb_R11}} (p {{mtb_R11_p_txt}}) shows that the change in cohort composition across event years does not explain the headline estimate, although the smaller sample widens the interval. Row R7 removes a linear pre-trend with Eq. (9). The MTB effect becomes {{mtb_R7}}, with a 95% interval from {{mtb_R7_lo}} to {{mtb_R7_hi}} that includes zero; it is negative because a continued run-up would have raised valuations further. For market capitalization, the same adjustment gives {{mcap_R7}} (p {{mcap_R7_p_txt}}), so scored firms' market value grew more slowly after coverage than a continued linear run-up implies. Row R8 conditions on pre-coverage growth, comparing scored firms with never-scored firms that grew at a similar rate and therefore share any tendency to revert. Its MTB effect is {{mtb_R8}} (p {{mtb_R8_p_txt}}). If part of the pre-coverage growth reflected temporary demand, as around index additions whose price effects partly reverse (Harris & Gurel, 1986), this comparison partly accounts for the reversal and shows no significant gain, but its interval, from {{mtb_R8_lo}} to {{mtb_R8_hi}}, admits gains of several percent. It conditions only on the growth of market capitalization from year g − 3 to year g − 1, and control firms matched on past growth can themselves revert, so it does not settle the reversal question.

Table IA5 of the Internet Appendix reports the bounds of Eq. (10), which follow the relative-magnitudes logic of Rambachan and Roth (2023). The largest change between consecutive pre-coverage MTB estimates is {{mtb_rm_dmax}}. The robust interval for the MTB effect is {{mtb_rm025_lo}} to {{mtb_rm025_hi}} with M̄ = 0.25, {{mtb_rm050_lo}} to {{mtb_rm050_hi}} with M̄ = 0.5, and {{mtb_rm100_lo}} to {{mtb_rm100_hi}} with M̄ = 1. Every interval includes zero, and none excludes moderate effects in either direction once departures from parallel trends of the size seen before coverage are allowed. The upper limit of the unadjusted interval, {{mtb_hi}}, lies just below a 5 percent increase in the MTB ratio ({{log105}} log points): any departure with M̄ of {{mtb_bd5}} or more admits a 5 percent increase, and any departure at all admits a positive effect. The post-coverage null and the absence of a positive effect are therefore both conditional on assumption (2).

## 5.5. Robustness and heterogeneity

The pre-specified checks in rows R1 to R4 of Table 3 leave the valuation result unchanged. The effect on log MTB is {{mtb_R1}} when not-yet-scored firms join the control group, as Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) propose, {{mtb_R2}} without the outcome-regression covariates of Sant'Anna and Zhao (2020), {{mtb_R3}} without the 2020 and 2021 cohorts, and {{mtb_R4}} when control firms below the size range of scored firms are dropped; none differs from zero at the 10% level. The size results are less stable. Under the size restriction, the market capitalization effect shrinks to {{mcap_R4}}, and the asset effect ranges from {{asset_R2}} without covariates to {{asset_R4}} under the size restriction, so we do not read the asset estimate in Table 2 as evidence that coverage causes growth.

The placebo in row R5 dates coverage three years before the first score and uses only years before the actual score. It yields {{mtb_R5}} on log MTB (p {{mtb_R5_p_txt}}) and {{mcap_R5}} on log market capitalization (p {{mcap_R5_p_txt}}). The placebo fails, and the failure restates the pre-coverage growth of Fig. 1 in a different form rather than adding independent evidence. Row R6 reports the static two-way fixed effects regression of Eq. (11), which attributes increases of {{mtb_R6}} in log MTB (p {{mtb_R6_p_txt}}) and {{mcap_R6}} in log market capitalization to coverage. Because it compares all post-coverage years with all earlier years, it counts part of the pre-coverage growth as an effect of the rating. Part of the gap between row R6 and the baseline reflects the covariates and the event window, as the larger estimate without covariates in row R2 shows; the remainder illustrates the biases of static two-way fixed effects regressions under staggered timing described by Goodman-Bacon (2021) and Baker et al. (2022).

Row R9 and Table IA1 of the Internet Appendix exclude one market at a time. The MTB effect ranges from {{mtb_lomo_min}} to {{mtb_lomo_max}} across the five samples and is {{mtb_R9}} without Malaysia (p {{mtb_R9_p_txt}}). The market capitalization effect turns to {{mcap_R9}} without Malaysian firms, so its negative baseline estimate reflects the large Malaysian cohorts of 2021 and 2022. Estimated market by market in Table IA3, the MTB effect is {{mtb_pm_MY}} in Malaysia, with {{mtb_pm_MY_ntr}} scored firms, and {{mtb_pm_TH}} in Thailand, with {{mtb_pm_TH_ntr}}. The estimates for Indonesia, the Philippines, and Singapore rest on few scored firms and have wide confidence intervals. The Singaporean estimate, of interest because reporting became mandatory there from financial year 2017 (Krueger et al., 2024; Singapore Exchange, 2016), is small and imprecise.

The last three rows of Table 3 split scored firms at the cohort median of their first ESG score. The MTB effect is {{mtb_high}} for firms that enter coverage with a high score and {{mtb_low}} for those with a low score. The difference of {{mtb_diff}} (standard error {{mtb_diff_se}}, p {{mtb_diff_p_txt}}) is not significant, and its minimum detectable value is {{mtb_diff_mde}}, so we find no evidence that the level of the first score matters for valuation, but the test cannot exclude moderate differences. The preference channel (Pedersen et al., 2021) and the evidence on favorable first coverage (Demiroglu & Ryngaert, 2010) predict such a difference; a weak signal, given that providers disagree (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Christensen et al., 2022), is one reason it may be absent. Because recent scores can be revised after publication (Sahin et al., 2023), the initial scores used in this split may differ from those that investors saw.

## 5.6. Economic magnitude

Under assumption (2), the largest increase compatible with the headline interval of Section 5.2 is of the same order as the announcement return of 4.86 percent that Demiroglu and Ryngaert (2010) report for the first analyst coverage of neglected stocks. It also exceeds the price increase of more than 3 percent that Harris and Gurel (1986) report for additions to the S&P 500, most of which reversed within about two weeks. Annual ratios measured at fiscal year-end would miss a short-lived effect of that size, so our estimates speak to lasting revaluation, as a permanent increase in investor recognition would produce (Chen et al., 2004; Merton, 1987), and not to temporary price pressure.

The pre-coverage run-up in Section 5.3 offers a second benchmark. Under assumption (2), a lasting valuation effect as large as half of the MTB run-up would lie just outside the headline interval. Any such effect is therefore small relative to the growth that precedes coverage, which is consistent with a provider that covers firms after they have become visible to investors (Section 2.2).
